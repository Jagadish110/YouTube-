Two teenagers built a seven billion dollar company out of a grocery app that failed. Then the stock market looked at it and said: four and a half.
This is how Zepto got so big, so fast, and why it still isn't on the stock market.
It starts in 2020. Aadit Palicha and Kaivalya Vohra are childhood friends who grew up in Dubai. They've just got into Stanford to study computer science.
Then the pandemic hits. They're stuck at home in Mumbai, and they can't get groceries delivered.
So, still teenagers, they build Kirana Kart. The idea is simple. Partner with local kirana shops and deliver their stock to your door.
It doesn't take off. Local shops run out of stock, they don't track inventory, and every order depends on a shopkeeper picking up the phone.
But the two of them don't go back to Stanford. An investor called Contrary offers to back them if they drop out. They take the deal.
In 2021 they shut Kirana Kart down and start again. New name: Zepto. New promise: groceries in ten minutes.
Here's the trick. Zepto doesn't use shops. It builds its own small warehouses, called dark stores, packed into dense neighbourhoods. No customers walk in. They only exist to pack orders fast.
Each dark store covers a small radius, so the rider only has a short distance to go. Aadit Palicha once pointed out that covering 1.8 kilometres in ten minutes means riding under fifteen kilometres an hour. The speed comes from where the store sits, not from how fast the rider goes.
And the stock is narrow on purpose. Not everything, just the few thousand items people actually reorder. Milk, bread, chips, chargers.
Investors loved it. Zepto was backed by Y Combinator, and by August 2023 it became India's first unicorn of the year.
Then the money came in waves. In 2024, six hundred and sixty-five million dollars at a three point six billion valuation. A few months later, three hundred and fifty million more at five billion.
In October 2025, a US pension fund called Calpers led a four hundred and fifty million dollar round. Zepto was now worth seven billion dollars.
On paper, the growth was insane. In the year ending March 2026, Zepto made twenty-two thousand six hundred crore rupees in revenue. It ran eleven hundred and thirty-nine dark stores. Nearly forty-eight million people ordered at least once that year.
In the last quarter of that year, it was handling over twenty-three lakh orders a day.
So why isn't it public?
Because revenue isn't profit. Ten minute delivery is expensive. You pay rent for stores in prime locations, you pay riders, and you pay discounts to win customers from Blinkit, Instamart and Amazon.
In the year before, Zepto's losses widened to over three thousand three hundred crore rupees. It was burning cash every quarter.
Zepto filed for its IPO in December 2025 and got approval from Sebi in May 2026. The plan was to list in July.
But Indian fund managers pushed back. Zepto only does quick commerce. It doesn't have a food delivery business like Swiggy or Zomato's parent, Eternal, to fall back on.
So in July 2026, Zepto pulled the brakes. Instead of listing, it raised around a thousand crore rupees privately, at a valuation of roughly four and a half billion dollars. That's about a third less than its peak.
The IPO was pushed back by two to three quarters.
Here's the part most people miss. Zepto isn't failing. Its cash burn came down, it says it has no debt, and it still has thousands of crores in the bank.
What happened is the gap between two kinds of money. Private investors pay for what a company could become. Public markets pay for what it earns.
Zepto proved that Indians want groceries in ten minutes. What it still has to prove is that ten minutes can make money.
And the two people who have to prove it are still in their mid-twenties.
