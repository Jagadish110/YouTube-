India's biggest Coca-Cola bottler is getting ready to go public. Its revenue has fallen almost eighteen percent in two years. So why list now?
Quick note: this is not investment advice. It's a breakdown of how the business works.
Meet Hindustan Coca-Cola Holdings. It doesn't own the Coca-Cola brand. It makes and delivers the drinks.
Here's how it works. The Coca-Cola Company sells the flavour base, called concentrate. Bottlers buy it, add water, sugar and fizz, bottle it, and get it into shops.
So the bottler earns on every bottle sold, but it depends on Coca-Cola for the concentrate and its price.
This bottler sells forty-one drinks across ten Indian states, through about seventeen lakh shops.
It runs fourteen plants of its own, plus eight partner plants.
In its states, Coca-Cola brands hold thirty-seven point two percent of the market by value. That's more than three times the nearest rival.
And its biggest brand isn't Coca-Cola. It's Thums Up.
Thums Up and Sprite each sold more than ten thousand crore rupees in India in the year to June twenty twenty-six.
Now, the falling revenue.
In financial year twenty twenty-four, revenue was about fourteen thousand and twenty crore rupees. By twenty twenty-six, it was eleven thousand five hundred and forty-nine crore.
But the company didn't just sell less. It handed whole regions, like Rajasthan, Bihar and the Northeast, to other Coca-Cola bottlers. That's called refranchising.
In the regions it kept, volume actually grew, from six hundred and fifty-eight million cases to about six hundred and eighty-five million.
And the latest quarter looks very different. From April to June twenty twenty-six, revenue was four thousand three hundred and ten crore rupees, and its operating margin rose to seventeen point two percent.
Now the IPO itself. It's an offer for sale. That means no new money goes to the company. Existing owners sell some of their shares.
The seller is Coca-Cola's own holding company. If it sells everything on offer, its stake falls from sixty percent to about forty-five.
The other forty percent belongs to Jubilant Beverages, which paid about eleven thousand seven hundred crore rupees for it.
That deal valued the whole company at about twenty-nine thousand crore rupees. Keep that number in mind when the IPO price comes out.
What could go wrong? The company's own papers flag a few risks.
Five territories bring in about three quarters of revenue. It depends on Coca-Cola for concentrate and pricing. And water: one plant stopped for fifteen days because of a water shortage.
There's also a quiet shift. From April to June, three out of four drinks it sold were low sugar or no sugar.
The price band and dates aren't out yet. When they are, the question is simple: what is a steady drinks business with thirty-seven percent of its market worth?
